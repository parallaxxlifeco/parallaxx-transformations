A woman I was working with shared with me: "I have made myself responsible that I need to know what is going to happen in the future. I have kind of an obligation to know."

Then she shared what she says to herself when things go wrong: "I often catch myself repeating this phrase to myself: I should have known that this is going to happen, even if there was no way that I could have known."

Many of the people I work with are like this. They're capable, they plan, they've usually thought it through before anyone else in the conversation has, and it's a big reason people rely on them. And when life goes a way no-one could have seen coming, they're often the first to blame themselves for it.

## Why do I feel like I need to know everything?

Often because at some point you made it your responsibility. Maybe no-one asked you to, and you took it on anyway, years ago, and over time it stops feeling like a choice and starts feeling like who you are.

She then described it like this: "It became like a natural part of how I saw myself and how I saw the world, that of course I have to know." For her it started in a relationship where she'd ended up responsible for someone else's life as well as hers, and she could see what that had done to the people around her too: "I'm kind of also denying their right to their own growth."

And to herself. "I have definitely done a lot of sabotage to my life with this belief."

She's not the only person I've heard this from. A woman in one of my groups shared that her pattern was control: "I want to control everything, I want it exactly the way I want it." A lot of that control is about knowing what's coming. If you know it in advance, you can get ahead of it, and getting ahead of things is what you're good at.

## Why do I always feel like I should have known?

Because "I should have known" is rarely where the sentence ends. When the woman I worked with shared it with me, I said to her, "It's almost like, see, you should have known. And then the next sentence is, you suck so much for not having known, or for having missed it. I expected more of you."

The first sentence sounds like it's about the future. The second one is about you, and how much you're worth when you get it wrong.

About a week later she added: "There might be some feelings of unworthiness behind that need of control."

I know this from my childhood. As I got deeper into personal development, I realised I developed this way because, from young, "when things didn't go the way it was anticipated, I felt like it became my fault. I was to blame." Now I can see it differently: "I probably wasn't to blame. Of course I wasn't to blame. I was just a young boy."

So you try to know everything in advance to protect yourself from that second belief. If you see it coming, you can't be blamed for missing it. That works for a long time. And then a day comes that there was no way of seeing coming, and you blame yourself anyway.

## Why do I always feel like I did something wrong?

Often because the voice telling you so is anger, directed at yourself. Many of us learned to keep our anger down. When we do, it shows up as passive aggression, as resentment, or as self-talk turned inward, which is what "I should have known" is.

When I explained this to the woman I was working with, she shared, "That's a form of anger, actually, that kind of self-talk." And I added, "Anger towards yourself."

You can't plan your way out of a feeling. You can make the list longer, check it again, think through another scenario, and the voice will still be there the next time you get caught out.

## Why does control feel safer than not knowing?

Because control gives you a sense of certainty, and certainty feels safe. You may hear me say, "Control gets a bit of a bad rap. The reason we want to control things is it gives us a sense of certainty… but it strangles the life out of the beauty of life, being present, being free."

It can also bring on overwhelm. I described overwhelm with this woman as the brain trying to attend to more than it can control. And she'd lived it during a hard stretch in her personal life: "In the beginning I was trying to control. I wanted to be in control of the whole situation and know exactly how I'm going to solve it. But then when I realized it's not possible, I let go and accepted the situation."

## Where does certainty come from?

From within you. I say this a lot: "The certainty isn't out there. We create the certainty within ourselves." And remember certainty is also an emotional experience, because if it was a logical experience, you could talk yourself into it.

Both women I've mentioned found this for themselves.

The woman I worked with privately started with the obligation to know. During that hard stretch, she shared, "It was kind of a moment of complete surrender. I just thought that whatever will happen, or wherever I will be the 1st of January, I will be okay, because I have this safety, this strong centre in myself."

The woman in my group started with wanting everything exactly the way she wanted it. Later on that same call, she described how it had changed: "Before, I needed to control to feel powerful. Now, I'm limited. I don't know what I don't know. I'm smart, but it doesn't mean I know everything. And I'm fine with not knowing."

Both of them are still capable. What changed is that being okay no longer depended on knowing first. You can't know what's coming. You can know you'll be okay when it comes.

## What comes up when you don't know?

Usually an urge. For the woman I worked with privately, the urge to go back and check was still there about two months later. When it came, she breathed and reminded herself that checking again wasn't in her plan. As I said to her, "So it's an urge. It's not a problem."

The step she chose for herself was this: "I want to pause and breathe and observe how I feel, and stay with that feeling and feel it fully." That's returning to the present, instead of trying to know the future.

And when "I should have known" comes, listen for the sentence after it, and get curious about that sentence instead of taking it as fact. The way I put it to the women in one of my groups: "Huh, that was an interesting thought I just said. Is that true?"

## Common questions

### Is needing to know everything a control issue?

Often, yes. Wanting to know what's coming is a way of being in control of it, and control gives you a sense of certainty. The woman in my group who shared that her pattern was control described the change as being fine with not knowing, while still being smart and capable.

### Isn't it good to be prepared?

Yes, and being prepared is a big reason people trust you. The difference is what happens when a situation comes that no-one could have prepared for. If you can say "there was no way I could have known" and mean it, the preparing is working for you. If you hear "I expected more of you", get curious about that voice.

### How is this different from feeling responsible for other people?

[How do I stop feeling responsible for others?](/insights/being-the-capable-one/is-it-my-responsibility-to-help/) is about whether a task or an outcome is yours to take on right now. This page is about the future: feeling you should have seen it coming, and blaming yourself when you didn't.

## If this resonates

If you're usually the one who's thought it through first, you already know how to look after the people and the things that matter to you. What comes next is trusting yourself to be okay in the moments you couldn't have seen coming, and noticing what you say to yourself when those moments come.

And it goes better with a few others around you who'll ask whether you really could have known, and who want to hear what you're looking forward to as well as what you're preparing for.
