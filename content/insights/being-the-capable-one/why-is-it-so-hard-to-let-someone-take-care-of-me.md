There was always enough in our house and never anything spare. So when I asked my mum for money I was asking for something that wasn't there, and I could see that on her face before she answered. It kept happening, so I stopped asking. I don't think I ever really started again.

What grew in the gap was being very good at not needing anything. I figured out early that if I build the thing everybody wants to be part of, I never really have to ask anyone for anything. It worked, and that's the reason it's still here thirty years later.

So the honest answer to the question is that there's nothing on your end to take it with. Somebody offers to look after something, and the reflex is faster than you are.

> Being provided for, being cared for, my automatic reaction is: no, I don't need you to do that for me, I'm happy for us to go halves. And I'm telling myself: receive, receive, receive.
> — a woman in one of these groups, describing her own reaction

## Not needing anything is a skill, and it paid

It developed for a reason, and it's been paying out for years, in standing and in never owing anybody. Your capability is not the problem here and it never was. What sits underneath it is that you stopped asking a long time ago, and being the one who can handle it grew up in that gap.

Which means being taken care of asks you to put down the one thing you're best at, in front of somebody, on purpose. Of course it's hard. It'd be strange if it wasn't.

A lot of the writing on this is pitched at somebody in crisis, and you're the one people call when they're in one, so it reads as though it's about somebody else.

## The discomfort is the behaviour going in

Every page that answers this question explains why it's hard and stops there. What fewer of them say is what happens when you do it anyway, and that part has an order to it.

A woman in one of these groups was asked what shifted in the moments she shut her mouth and received. This is what she said.

> The first thing I notice is how uncomfortable I am. How ingrained it is, even when you know the behaviour you need to change, it doesn't make it easier. So I've got to fight the discomfort. Then, proud of myself for doing what I know I should do. And then eventually: oh, actually, this is kind of nice. That feeling of being more supported.
> — a woman in one of these groups, on what changed when she received

Uncomfortable because it's unfamiliar, go through it, then the reward. Three stages, and the trouble is that each one gets read as something else.

| The stage | What it feels like | What it gets read as |
|---|---|---|
| Somebody offers you something | Uncomfortable, because it's unfamiliar | Proof you don't want it |
| You take it anyway | Proud of yourself for doing it | Performing, so it doesn't count |
| It happens a few more times | Oh, this is kind of nice | The first time the support feels like yours |

If you only ever get as far as stage one, the discomfort is all the evidence you have, and it argues for going halves. That's the reason this stays stuck. You're not wrong about the feeling. You're reading it as a verdict when it's the start of something unfamiliar, and a verdict is the easier of the two to believe, right?

## Receiving on your own terms is not receiving

There's a version of this that looks like progress and is the same reflex again. You're open to it, but only in this way, and this way, and this way. Only if you picked it, only if it arrives when you planned for it, only if you can be sure you deserved it.

Receiving on your own terms is not receiving. One participant had turned it into something she could fail at, so the gift would arrive and then an internal audit would run on whether she had received it correctly. Another described it as wanting to control not only what she was getting but how she was going to get it, and then said that when she chills the f*** out, things come in. Two of them recognised the same thing on the same call and both were surprised by it.

So the rep isn't just taking it. It's taking it in the form it arrives in, at the time it arrives, from the person offering, without grading yourself afterwards.

## What's underneath it is often not wanting to be a burden

When people say why they can't take it, the reason is rarely about the other person's money or time. Another participant shared that she used to suppress her emotions a lot and not show them, because she didn't want to feel like a burden to others.

She wasn't suppressing because she couldn't feel anything. She was suppressing to protect everybody else from her. It's the same caretaking you do all day, pointed at your own feelings, and it means the kindest thing you know how to do is need nothing.

Which is why the fix is never to care less. In the same week she allowed herself to feel, and to receive love from others, and to cry in front of people, which she said was uncomfortable as well. Same word again.

## No amount of inner work substitutes for being received

This is the objection that comes up a lot in this territory, and it's fair: I've done a lot on this already. Usually true. Many of the people I sit with can tell me their own pattern better than I can, when it started and who taught it to them, and a lot of them have read the book and done the course as well.

What's missing is being received by another person, and that's the one thing no amount of time on your own delivers, because it needs somebody else in it. You can't receive something you won't give yourself yet, and until you know how to receive it, the people around you have nothing to give.

## Try this

Try this: next time somebody offers you something, take it in the form they offered it and notice what you feel first?

Then the level above receiving, which is asking before anyone offers. One woman in these groups moved from rejecting everything and not being able to take a compliment, to saying it up front: now I express it when I'm positive, not after I've realised I haven't received it. If you want a sentence to use, this one works: I'm always on and always thinking about work, and something I'd really love support with is just being present, without needing to be productive.

That's the discomfort you've been reluctant to go near, and the reps get built around exactly that.

## Common questions

### Why do I feel guilty when someone does something for me?

Because the thing you're best at is not being a cost to anybody, so somebody spending on you reads as a debt you now owe rather than as something they wanted to do, and the guilt is that ledger opening rather than evidence you did anything wrong.

### Is this hyper-independence?

That's the word many people use for it, and it describes the behaviour accurately, though it can make a skill you developed for good reasons sound like a defect, and the more useful question is what it's protecting other people from.

### I've done a lot of inner work on this. Why is it still hard?

Because understanding where it came from is a different thing from doing it in front of somebody, and the second one only comes with repetition, which is the reason it stays hard long after it stops being mysterious.

### How do I ask for something instead of waiting to be offered?

Say what you want while you still feel good rather than after the disappointment, keep it to one sentence, and make it about what you want more of rather than about what somebody failed to notice.

### Does the discomfort ever go away?

It gets smaller and it stops being the loudest thing in the moment, and what changes first is what you do with it, which is take the thing anyway and let the rest catch up.

## The thing you can't do on your own

Everything else in this you can practise alone. This one you can't, because the skill is what you do when somebody is holding something out to you, and that needs the other person there. And the first few goes are easier with people who aren't going to let you split the bill to make it comfortable again.
