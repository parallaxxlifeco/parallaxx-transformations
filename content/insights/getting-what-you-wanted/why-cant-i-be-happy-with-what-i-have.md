A woman in a group I run shared that she'd felt "so guilty for wanting more, right?" And the voice that came with the guilt went, "Oh my god, why can't I just settle?"

From the outside, her life looked good. She'd spent the past decade doing a lot of work on herself. Then she went through her acknowledgements for the week so quickly that I said to her, "It was almost like, okay, I'll try to keep up."

So I slowed her down with a question: "You notice all of these acknowledgements. What does it bring you inside?" Her first answer was, "Like a feeling of pride," and then she paused. Then she said, "Like this moment, I feel so grateful and privileged." And she cried. "It's the gratitude cry," she said.

Everything she'd listed was there before I asked. This time she let herself feel it.

## Why can't I be happy with what I have?

The truth is that you can be, and be allowed to keep wanting more too. Many of us tick off what we already have and move past it, so we know it in our head but don't feel it as an experience. I shared with the group: "It's internal state creation that allows these experiences to land, to go from a talk in the head to an emotion in the body." Being happy with what you have is the emotion in the body. The list is the talk in the head.

She saw it herself by the end of that share: "So it's so freeing to see that all the work I've been doing for the past decade is not rewarded in those things I thought I needed or wanted, it's rewarded in the way I feel inside."

I know this from my own life too. I had a stretch of travelling with a schedule so tight that getting excited for the next thing meant I wasn't enjoying being where I was. I shared it with the men in one of my groups: "I've arrived. I've done it, but I'm not getting to appreciate it." It's so easy to get tied up in the logistics and the doing, going through the motions of it all, that I forget to bring the appreciation, the gratitude, for the moments and the now.

## Why do I feel guilty for wanting more?

It can come from wanting more than the people around you, especially when some of them have chosen security instead. So the wanting starts to look like the problem, and the voice telling you to be grateful sounds like the sensible one. For the woman I mentioned at the start, the guilt went back a long way.

This is how she described the years before: "For the majority of it there was so much guilt and shame, that I want more, or I do more, or I'm different." And this is how she described where she is now, on the same call: "And now I feel compassion, because it's not easy to be where I am. But I also feel gratitude for where I am."

She still wants more. What she stopped doing was treating the wanting as something to be ashamed of, and once she did, she could feel grateful for where she is.

She also stopped needing the people around her to want the same thing. When they told her she was brave for leaning into uncertainty, and that they liked their security, her answer was, "I like it too, but I don't want to die comfortable, right?" And she doesn't have to stay unhappy because some of them are: "It's not like everybody else is miserable, so I have to stay in that misery. It's more like: I deserve the peace of mind."

A week later she was feeling guilty about having what she wanted. She came in with this question: "Why I get to enjoy something where other people are miserable?" And this one: "I didn't work hard for it, so why I get to experience that?"

She wasn't the only woman in that group asking this. Another shared that she feels she has to work hard "to get something that I deserve," and then asked, "Is it okay to accept this one, because I'm not doing enough work?"

So if you've felt guilty for wanting more, you can feel guilty for having it too, and then you can't enjoy what you wanted once you've got it.

## Why am I never satisfied with anything?

Often because you set the next target before you've let yourself feel the last result. The woman who felt guilty for wanting more described the harsh voice in her head as "I'm supposed to have better outcomes, faster, bigger, God knows how many zeros, these vanity metrics."

You can see this in small things too. She had an idea on a Saturday and it was ready by the Monday, and her reaction was, "48 hours, too long." Then she said, "I'm like, okay, I've got to chill the f out here, because it's just getting a little bit crazy."

I see this with to-do lists. A lot of people write ten things, get through five, and tell themselves they failed because they only got through fifty per cent. So write five. Cross all five off. And then, as I said to a group member, "I get to go to the beach now."

## I have everything I need, so why don't I feel it?

Because listing what you're grateful for and feeling it are two different things. You can tick off everything you're grateful for and still not feel it. And I'll put my hand up here, gratitude lists haven't done much for me.

I said this to the men in one of my groups: "People say, list out what you're grateful for each day. I was never very good at that. I'm still not that good at it. Because I'm sitting there like, I'm grateful for my heart beating that's keeping me alive, or all these basic things. And I'm like, am I really grateful for that? Sounds a bit of bullshit. I'd like to be, but I don't really feel it. It sounds like I'm just trying to blow smoke up my own ass."

What works for me is getting specific. I pick an area I care about and think of experiences I loved in it, like this: "I'm so grateful that I get to come and hang out with some epic brothers every week, share what it is that I know, and they get to share with each other." When I feel that in my body, I add another, and another. "I do at least three. If I can get to five, I do that."

| | Ticked off | Felt |
|---|---|---|
| What it sounds like | My health, my heart beating | "I'm so grateful that I get to come and hang out with some epic brothers every week" |
| How quickly it goes | As fast as you can say it | Slow enough to notice it in your body |
| What comes after | "What's next?" | Another, until you've got three to five |

## What does it bring you inside?

That's the question I asked the woman who felt guilty for wanting more, and it's the question to ask yourself when the list starts coming fast. Stop at the first good thing and stay with it until you can say what it brings you inside.

A few weeks after that call, she shared, "I'm celebrating myself all the time. There's nothing stopping me on that. But do I enjoy that celebration? That's a separate story."

Then she told us about a gorgeous restaurant, with her notebook, a book and her phone on the table, and her first thought was, "What is on the agenda?" Instead she said to herself, "Let me just enjoy the food, the fish that I ordered, and the view of the sea. Let me just sit with it and enjoy." She acknowledged herself "just for sitting and doing nothing, which is crazy uncomfortable. Just sitting, and like, 'What's next? What's next?'"

And she shared, "Hopefully in a few weeks I'll start loving it, instead of chasing that excitement and thrill that doesn't allow me to enjoy the moment."

Her share reminded me of a mentor coach I had a few years ago. I messaged him and he said, "I'm just practising my boredom." And I laughed, like, who practises being bored? But if we can sit with the boredom, then we can sit with presence, and when we're present we get to feel what we have.

So when things are good, as I said on a call once, "allow them to be fucking good, and enjoy it. Appreciate it." You get to keep the wanting more too.

## Common questions

### Is wanting more ungrateful?

Wanting more and being grateful can both be true at once. The guilt often comes from treating them as a trade, where wanting more must mean you don't appreciate what you've got. The woman I mentioned at the start wanted more, and she cried with gratitude on the same call.

### Should I just be grateful for what I have?

Try it, and if a list of basics doesn't do anything for you, it didn't for me either. Make it specific. Pick experiences you loved, in an area you care about, and stay with each until you feel it in your body. Three is a good start.

### How is this different from feeling empty after achieving a goal?

[Why do I feel empty after achieving a goal I worked years for?](/insights/getting-what-you-wanted/why-do-i-feel-empty-after-achieving-a-goal/) is about a goal measured by how it looks from the outside, where you never said what experience you wanted from it. This page is for when the life is good and the experiences are there, and you move past them before you've felt them.

## If this resonates

If you're the one who keeps going for more, you already know how to show up for what you want and go and get it. What comes next is giving what's already here that same attention, for long enough to feel it.

And it goes better with a few others around you who'll stop you mid-list to ask what it brings you inside, and who want to hear what you're enjoying as well as what's next.
