I have done this twice, and the second time was worse, because by then I had nowhere left to put the blame.

The first time was a middle management job in tech. Good six figure income, business development, international travel, corporate card, a high-rise apartment in Sydney and another in Melbourne. Everything I could have wanted on the salary I had. I got there and I was burnt out, lost, confused, wondering why I felt so trapped by the thing I had spent years building toward.

So I did what many of us do with that feeling. I set a bigger goal.

## What the second time looked like

I quit and went into remote sales consulting, earning ten, sometimes eleven grand a week. That money bought what I said I wanted, which was freedom, location independence and time. I moved to Bali. Tropical island, everything sorted, at least visually.

And I felt worse than before.

I had everything sorted, and when I looked around I realised I was not the one getting invited anywhere, because I hadn't been initiating anything myself. When I was at things, it felt like a performance to impress people so I could fit in.

That was the low point, and it was also where things started to change. Everywhere I went, they were my problems. The common denominator was me.

## Why doing more stops working

The belief underneath both of those years was simple. More action equals more progress, and progress eventually gets you what you want.

The trap in it is that it is half true. More action does produce results. What it does not do is produce an experience, and an experience is the thing I was after. Doing does not help you feel. So the harder I worked, the more evidence I collected that I was moving in the right direction, and the further I got from anything I would have described as the life I wanted.

But what took me the longest to see was this: the doing was the distraction. Staying busy meant I never had to sit with the question of what I was building it all for.

> The faster I hustled, the more I could tell myself I was moving toward my dreams. It was the very thing keeping me stuck.
> — Daniel Lawson

## Was the goal ever yours?

The dream I was chasing was the laptop lifestyle. Location independence, do what I want, when I want, with whoever I want. Beach, cocktails, never really working.

I inherited that. I picked it up without much extra thought, and then I spent four years and a lot of energy getting there. When I got there it was boring. If you ask me now what I want, it's a long way from that.

So I was chasing a goal that was someone else's.

Many of us never define the target ourselves. We take a goal that other people can see, like the title, the number, the apartment or the island, because a goal like that can be shown, and a felt one can't. Then we hit it, and there's no-one to show it to who makes it mean anything, and the emptiness comes right on time.

## What was underneath the goal?

When I got honest about Bali, what I found underneath was that I had no real friendships, and the reason was me. I did not initiate. I did not invite people. I did not think about others much. I waited for everyone to come to me, and I kept just enough surface connection going to not feel lonely, until there was too much of it to hide from.

Achieving more wasn't going to change that. What changes it is wanting a specific experience with the people around you and then asking for it, which is the exact skill many of us stopped practising a long time ago.

So the sequence I use now, and sometimes I notice myself skipping it:

1. Say what experience you want, not the outcome. Not the income, the feeling of the week that income is supposed to buy.
2. Check whose it is. If you cannot remember deciding on it, you probably did not.
3. Ask someone for a small, specific thing they could say no to. This is the hard one, and it's the one that changes things.
4. See what happens, and let that be the data rather than the plan.

The reason step three is hard is that a man who provides cannot be turned down, and a man who asks can. Providing is safer. It's also why you can be indispensable to everyone in your life and still feel like no-one knows you.

## Common questions

### Does this mean I should stop setting goals?

No. Keep the goals, and put a felt experience beside each one: what a Tuesday looks like when you have it, who is there, what you are doing at seven in the evening. A goal with no experience attached is a number, and hitting a number is what comes up empty.

### Is this burnout?

Burnout and this overlap, and they are worth separating. Burnout is about load, and it eases when the load eases. This does not ease with rest, because it is about direction. If you take two weeks off and come back to the same flatness, rest was not the missing piece.

### How long does it take to shift?

It changes in small doses rather than all at once. Saying a want that's only for you, and asking someone for a specific thing, changes more in a fortnight than a year of setting better targets. It took me a lot longer than that, mostly because I had no-one around me doing the same thing.

## If this resonates

If you've hit a goal you worked years for, you already know how to commit to something and see it through. What comes next is pointing that same commitment at an experience you want, and asking somebody for a small piece of it.

And it goes better with a few others around you who ask what you want. I did both of mine without that, and it took me years longer than it needed to, because I'd stopped asking myself a long time before.
