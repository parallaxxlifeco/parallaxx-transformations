I have done this twice, and the second time was worse, because by then I had nowhere left to put the blame.

The first time was a middle management job in tech. Good six figure income, business development, international travel, corporate card, a high-rise apartment in Sydney and another in Melbourne. Everything I could have wanted on the salary I had. I got there and I was burnt out, lost, confused, wondering why I felt so trapped by the thing I had spent years building toward.

So I did what most of us do with that feeling. I set a bigger goal.

## What the second time looked like

I quit and went into remote sales consulting, earning ten, eleven thousand a week. That money bought the thing I said I wanted — freedom, location independence, time. I moved to Bali. Tropical island, everything sorted, at least visually.

And I felt worse than before.

Sixty hours a week, six days a week, building something that mattered to me. Then on the seventh, when I finally pulled my head out of it, I looked around and realised I was not the one getting invited anywhere. When I was at things, it felt like a performance to impress people so I could fit in.

That was the low point, and it was also the useful part. Everywhere I went, they were my problems. The common denominator was me.

## Why doing more stops working

The belief underneath both of those years was simple. More action equals more progress, and progress eventually gets you what you want.

The trap in it is that it is half true. More action does produce results. What it does not do is produce an experience, and an experience is the thing I was after. Doing does not help you feel. So the harder I worked, the more evidence I collected that I was moving in the right direction, and the further I got from anything I would have described as the life I wanted.

But what took me the longest to see was this: the doing was the distraction. Staying busy meant I never had to sit with the question of what I was building any of it for.

> The faster I hustled, the more I could tell myself I was moving toward my dreams. It was the very thing keeping me stuck.
> — Daniel Lawson

## Was the goal ever yours?

This is the part that changes things, and it stings a bit.

The dream I was chasing was the laptop lifestyle. Location independence, do what I want, when I want, with whoever I want. Beach, cocktails, never really working.

I inherited that. It was sold to me, and I picked it up without examining it, and then I spent four years and a lot of energy getting there. When I got there it was boring. If you ask me now what I want, it is not that, and it was never that.

So it was not a lie exactly. I was chasing the wrong thing, and the wrong thing was someone else's.

Most of us never define the target ourselves. We take a version that is legible to other people — the title, the number, the apartment, the island — because a legible goal can be shown, and a felt one cannot. Then we hit it, and there is nobody to show it to who makes it mean anything, and the emptiness arrives right on time.

## What gets named instead

When I got honest about Bali, the thing I found underneath was not a goal. It was that I had no real friendships, and the reason was me. I did not initiate. I did not invite people. I did not think about others much. I waited for everyone to come to me, and I kept just enough surface connection going to not feel lonely, until there was too much of it to hide from.

That is not something you fix by achieving anything. You fix it by wanting something specific from the people around you and then asking for it, which is the exact skill many of us stopped practising a long time ago.

So the sequence I use now, and I still catch myself skipping it:

1. Name the experience you want, not the outcome. Not the income, the feeling of the week that income is supposed to buy.
2. Check whose it is. If you cannot remember deciding on it, you probably did not.
3. Ask someone for something. A small, specific, decline-able thing. This is the hard one, and it is the one that moves.
4. See what happens, and let that be the data rather than the plan.

The reason step three is hard is that a man who provides cannot be turned down, and a man who asks can. Providing is safer. It is also why you can be indispensable to everyone in your life and still feel like nobody knows you.

## Common questions

### Does this mean I should stop setting goals?

No. Keep the goals, and put a felt experience beside each one — what a Tuesday looks like when you have it, who is there, what you are doing at seven in the evening. A goal with no experience attached is a number, and hitting a number is the part that comes up empty.

### Is this burnout?

Burnout and this overlap, and they are worth separating. Burnout is about load, and it eases when the load eases. This does not ease with rest, because it is about direction. If you take two weeks off and come back to the same flatness, rest was not the missing piece.

### How long does it take to shift?

It moves in small doses rather than in one turn. Naming one thing you want for yourself, and asking one person for one specific thing, changes more in a fortnight than a year of setting better targets. It took me a lot longer than that, mostly because I had nobody around me doing the same thing.

## If this resonates

The pattern underneath all of it is that you stopped asking for things, got very good at not needing anything, and then forgot you wanted anything in the first place. It is worth going after, and it is much harder to do on your own.

I did both of mine on my own, and it took me years longer than it needed to. Not because the answers were complicated. Because there was nobody around me asking what I wanted, and I'd stopped asking myself a long time before that.
