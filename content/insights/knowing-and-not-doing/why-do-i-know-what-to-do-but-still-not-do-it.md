There's a man in my group who spent nearly a year not asking for what he wanted, and it was the same non-ask, running in three different areas of his life at once.

And each time he had a reason. She was busy. He was travelling. It wasn't the right week. Next month would be better once the thing at work settled down.

Each of those was true, by the way, which is why this is so hard to catch.

## The reason is always a good reason

If the excuse was rubbish you'd have spotted it years ago. From the inside it looks like respect and patience, being considerate of somebody else's week and realistic about yours, so it doesn't feel like avoidance at all.

I call it the considerate delay, and it's one of the most well-behaved things we do to ourselves.

I see this in many of the men I work with. A group member does it through consideration. Another shuts the thought down before it finishes forming, so there's nothing to delay because the want never got said, even to himself. A third fixes the problem instead of asking whether the problem is his to fix, and another does it himself because he trusts his own eye for detail over anyone else's. And some go and learn more about it first.

Consideration, realism, responsibility, standards and preparation are all respectable reasons, and the same thing is happening underneath each of them.

## It's never the thing you think is stopping you

This took me way longer to believe than I would have liked.

Whatever you said was the obstacle, the obstacle isn't it. I've watched a man clear the exact thing he said was in the way, and within about a fortnight there was a new one, just as reasonable as the last.

> every time you want something, there's always something that's preventing you from doing it now, and it's always a do-it-later
> — Daniel Lawson

I still do this. A few weeks ago I had a conversation with my partner that didn't finish properly, and I told myself I'd come back to it the next day. Then a few days went past and I noticed myself thinking, do I even need to have it now? Maybe I just wait and see if it comes up again on its own.

It's the same thing, running in a man who coaches this. It doesn't leave. You just get faster at noticing it.

## So what is the delay for?

I always say procrastination is the solution protecting you from your problem.

The not-doing is solving a problem for you, and usually that problem is the risk of finding out. Because right now, in the not-asking, you get to keep the answer open. She might say yes. They might say yes. The thing might work. As long as you haven't asked, every outcome is still available, and none can be taken off the table.

Ask, and you find out. And finding out is what the protective side of you is working very hard to prevent.

It's doing a wonderful job. It keeps you feeling fine today. It just also keeps you exactly where you are.

## What changes this?

You've already got the insight. If understanding this was going to fix it, reading this far would have done it.

What changes this is a rep: a small, specific ask that someone could say no to, made to a real person, this week.

Small matters. People hear this and go straight to the biggest conversation in their life, which is a great way to guarantee it doesn't happen. That's the elusive delay again.

So the three questions I'd put to you:

1. What's the thing you want that you haven't said?
2. Who specifically would you have to say it to?
3. What's the simplest way you could say it this week, where a no would still be okay?

Then go and find out what happens, rather than what you predict will happen.

The guy I mentioned at the start eventually asked. Took him nearly a year to get there and about ninety seconds to do. And he got a no.

And afterwards he shared, "I don't see it as rejection, I see it as clarity."

What's on the other side of the ask is information, whichever answer you get.

## Common questions

### Is this just procrastination?

It looks identical from the outside and it works differently underneath. Regular procrastination is about a task you don't fancy doing. This is about an outcome you're not ready to find out about, which is why productivity systems don't change it.

### How do I tell a good reason from an excuse?

Test it by clearing it. If you remove the reason you gave and a new one shows up within a couple of weeks, the reason was never the reason. That's the easiest test I know.

### What if I ask and they say no?

Then you know, which is more than you had before. The fear is often bigger than the event. Many of the men I work with share this afterwards, in nearly the same words: the fear of it was far worse than the thing happening.

## If this is you

If you're the one who's considerate of everybody else's week, you already know how to read what people need and pick the right moment. What comes next is giving your own want a date, and saying it to someone this week.

That goes better with other people around you, because in private you can talk yourself out of it and no-one ever knows. That's the reason I run groups as well as working with people privately. Without anyone else in on it, the not-asking never has to be admitted, so it just carries on. In a group somebody is expecting to hear what happened this week, and that turns out to be the difference between knowing the pattern and interrupting it.
