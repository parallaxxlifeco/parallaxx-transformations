For years I told people I needed a woman who would command my attention. I didn't want her to demand it, I wanted her to command it.

I was pretty pleased with that line. It felt like self-knowledge. I'd figured out something true about myself, I could say it clearly, and it explained a lot about why relationships had gone the way they'd gone.

Then a guy said to me, well, aren't you just outsourcing your responsibility?

Took me over a year to get what he meant. What I had was a job description for somebody who hadn't shown up yet. I'd written a very articulate account of why the thing I wasn't doing was somebody else's job.

And that's awareness for you. It can be completely accurate and still be working for the other side.

## Describing it comes easily

Many of the people I work with can tell me their pattern better than I can, when it started and who taught it to them, and a lot of them have read the book, done the course, maybe sat with a therapist for a few years and got good at the language.

And they're still in it. Me too, on the things I haven't sorted yet.

A lot of my industry is built on that gap and doesn't mention it. Read the textbooks, learn the strategies, analyse it well enough and you get to call it handled. It's a bypass. It's a very comfortable one, because analysing is what you're already good at, and it produces the feeling of progress without any of the exposure that change comes with.

I've said the same thing to the same man twice, months apart, and watched him have the same realisation both times. Both times it was real, and both times nothing changed. At some point you have to stop being impressed by the insight.

## So why am I good at thinking and bad at doing?

Because thinking rarely costs you anything.

When you analyse something you stay in the one place where you have never lost. You're capable, you're articulate, you can see a problem from several angles at once, and thinking is where being clever pays out.

Doing is different. Doing puts you in front of people where you might be wrong. It has a result, and the result might not be the one you wanted, and then you'd have to sit in that.

So of course the analysis gets more sophisticated every year. You're practising the safe one.

And learning is one of the most respectable ways to not start, because few people will ever challenge you on it. Read another book, do another course, wait until you understand it properly, and each of those looks like diligence from the outside. From the inside it's the same delay, and this time it looks sensible.

## The pattern is a solution

You may be treating the pattern like an error, something you'd stop doing if only you understood it properly. So you understand it harder, with more reading and more reflection.

But the pattern is a solution. It got built for a specific reason, it protected you from a real hurt at the time, and it's still protecting you now.

> it's the protective parts of ourselves doing a wonderful job of not helping us cause more pain within ourselves so we can feel good in the moment, but it keeps us stuck
> — Daniel Lawson

Which means understanding it doesn't retire it. If anything it gets more sophisticated, because now you can use your own language to explain it away. The most self-aware people I know have the most articulate avoidance.

Mine is storytelling. One of the easiest ways we protect ourselves from the answers we say we're looking for is over-complicating it by telling stories, and I know that because it's one of my biggest protective mechanisms. I can talk my way around problems. I can confuse the shit out of coaches, because I can lead them astray just so they can't see who I am and what my real challenges are.

What a lot of people do when they finally see it is go, right, I've got it now, I'll just try harder. And trying harder against a protective side of yourself is like arguing with your immune system. It's doing what it was built to do, and it's been doing it longer than your plan has existed.

## Why do I overthink instead of doing?

So many of the things that feel stuck are stuck because you keep going round them in your head.

And the instinct, when something is stuck in your head, is to think about it more carefully, which keeps you going round.

What breaks it is moving quicker than is comfortable, just before the point where your mind wants another lap. Because the extra lap isn't producing new information, it's producing the feeling of having done something, and that feeling is what you're going back for.

Watch yourself next time. The fourth time round a problem, are you learning anything? Or does it just feel better than the alternative, which is going and finding out?

## So what changes this?

The moment before the pattern runs, those couple of seconds where you feel the pull and there's a choice you don't normally clock. Your body usually gets there first, a beat ahead of your thinking. Tight chest, the urge to change subject, the sudden very good reason to check your phone.

That moment is where you can choose differently. After it, you're explaining what already happened.

So the practice is small and repetitive and nothing like the insight work you're used to:

1. Find your clue. Many of us have one, and mine's above.
2. Notice it while it's happening, rather than three hours later. You don't have to stop it yet.
3. Do the other thing, a single time. Say the sentence, ask the question, stay in the conversation a little longer than is comfortable.
4. Notice what happened, rather than what you predicted.

Those are four reps. Reps are boring, which is why few people write about them and why the market keeps selling you the realisation instead.

## Acknowledge yourself for the attribute, not the outcome

What I almost never see, though is this. Someone capable goes and does a genuinely hard thing, and then says, I'm proud of myself for that.

It's rare. Successful, independent people hit the target and move straight to the next one, and the doing never gets banked, so it never becomes evidence of anything.

So when you do a rep, acknowledge what it required of you rather than what it produced. So rather than "I sent the message", it's "I said what I wanted without softening it first, and I didn't take it back when it went quiet for an hour."

That's the sentence that builds something. The outcome is out of your hands anyway. What it took from you is yours.

## Common questions

### So is self-awareness useless?

It's necessary, and on its own it isn't enough. Without it you can't see the moment coming at all. With it and nothing else you get a very good seat for watching yourself do the same thing again.

### Isn't preparation important?

Yes, and there's a point where it stops being preparation. The test is whether the next hour of thinking will change what you do. If you already know what you'd do and you're refining how you'd feel about it, you've moved past preparing.

### Why does it feel like I'm going backwards?

Because you can see it now, and you couldn't before. The behaviour hasn't got worse, the visibility has got better, which is uncomfortable and is also the first honest measurement you've had.

### I've tried forcing myself and it doesn't stick. Now what?

Forcing tends to fail because the target was too big. Shrink it until it's almost embarrassing, then do that. Momentum built from small completed things lasts. Momentum built from a single heroic effort usually fails the first week you're tired.

## If this is you

If you can describe your pattern better than most people can describe theirs, you already have the hardest thing to learn, which is seeing yourself clearly. What comes next is using that in the moment before, while it's still happening.

And that goes better with other people in the conversation, which is why I run groups as well as working with people privately. Somebody else sees it a few seconds before you do, and says so, and then it's in front of you while it's still happening rather than three hours later.
