I spent about a decade building a life where I could do what I wanted, when I wanted, from wherever I wanted. And I got it. Remote income, no-one to report to, weeks where more money came in than I knew what to do with.

Then I had the time and no idea how to fill it. I sat in it for a while and went back to distracting myself, because that at least was something to do.

It took me years to figure out what was missing. The money and the freedom were fine, and the plan was somebody else's, which I'll come back to. What had gone was the wanting.

## Why nothing comes when somebody asks what you want

Maybe you've been practising a different question for a very long time. What is going wrong, what needs sorting, what everybody around you needs this week. Ask for any of that and you could go all day without pausing. Then the question turns and points at you and there's nothing there.

I run an exercise on this and the same thing happens nearly every time:

> Most people are professionals, really dialled in on what they don't want. And every time I ask, okay, well that's what you don't want, what do you want instead, it trips off a lot of people.
> — Daniel Lawson

You are fluent in what you don't want, and out of practice at the other one. Those are two different skills and only one of them gets used.

I found my edge with this sitting with a coach who asked me what I valued in a relationship. I gave him trust, honesty, respect, loyalty. He said, with respect, those are baselines. If you don't have those you don't have a relationship at all. What's uniquely important to you?

And I went blank. Sat there with my coffee going cold and had no idea.

## What I don't know usually means

Often it can be that you got asked a question far too big. You got asked about your whole life. What do you want. No-one can answer that one, because there's nothing to take hold of in it, so nothing comes back.

A group member had been running a well known goal-setting method at night, the one where you write the result first and then the actions under it. They could not fill in the first box. Their words were that they didn't know what their objectives were anymore, because they had been so focused on doing, doing, doing.

The doing was working fine and it never stopped for a second. It was the box above it that was empty.

That's what makes this one so disorienting. Everything else about you is working. You are the person who gets asked, who gets relied on, who gets it done. And then a regular question comes at you and produces nothing at all, and it feels like a fault in you rather than a skill you stopped using.

## Where the wanting went

Mine went early and I can still see it.

Every time I asked my mum for something as a kid, I remember the disappointment on her face. It was a single-mother house and there wasn't much money in it. Just enough for the things we needed and nothing spare. So when I asked for something on top of that it wasn't there to give, and she felt that, and I watched her feel it. So I stopped asking, and I got very good at not needing anything.

Many of us do something like that, and then we forget we ever wanted anything. You stop asking first, and the forgetting comes later and for free.

What I hear from people now is the same thing underneath.

One participant said they don't fight for things anymore, they just gave up. They gave up on the wanting, and they had been calling it harmony for years.

Another put it as not having a direct line to what you want, because there are always other people to consider on the way to it. By the time you've routed a want through everyone it touches, it doesn't look like yours anymore.

A third described shutting the thought down before it had finished forming. Told themselves there was nothing there for them, without ever having looked. Coming back to it later, they said the thing they'd been calling realism was fear.

| What it sounds like from inside | What is going on |
|---|---|
| I'm easygoing, I don't mind | The asking stopped a long time ago |
| I just want everyone sorted | The other question is the practised one |
| I don't know, I'd have to think about it | The question was about your whole life |
| I don't want to make a thing of it | A want that could be refused feels like a risk |

## So what do we do instead?

Three things, and all of them are smaller than the problem sounds.

**First, ask it at a size you can answer.** Ask it for the next month, the next two months, this quarter. What are you going for, and what's the first thing that comes. Then go again on whatever showed up: what's the specific want inside that. Then once more: what's the want inside that for you, rather than for everyone who leans on you. One participant described it as taking a wall down step by step, and that's about right. The first answer is often everyone else's answer. The third tends to be yours.

**Second, write a want down every day with nothing attached to it.** A group member keeps a line at the bottom of their journal, every day, underneath the gratitude and the intentions: I want what I want. No plan bolted onto it, no case built for why it's reasonable, no checking whether it's available. Just the wanting, for its own sake. Do that for a year and you have 365 of them, and they get bigger as you go, because the muscle comes back.

**Third, say a want to somebody who could say no.** This is what changes things, and it's what many of us skip.

A want no-one could refuse is a preference. It costs nothing and proves nothing. The one that counts has a person on the other end of it who might decline, which is why we route around it and provide something instead. Sometimes I notice myself doing the swap. I could ask somebody for what I want, and instead I go and build the thing, or offer them something of mine, and the want I started with never gets said. It looks generous from the outside. From the inside it's a way of keeping the answer off the table.

## Common questions

### What if I do all that and still come up empty?

Then you say so. A group member answered a direct question with "I don't know that answer", didn't sugar coat it, and that was a better session than any invented answer would have produced. Not knowing is workable. Pretending to know is what stalls.

### Isn't this just goal setting?

Goal setting starts after this. Every method you have tried assumes there is something in the first box, and this is the problem of the first box being empty. That's why productivity advice bounces off you, because you can already execute.

### I know exactly what I want for my family and my work. Why can't I do it for myself?

Because those wants have a legitimate reason attached and yours has to stand up on its own. A want for somebody else comes pre-justified, so it's allowed through. Wanting a thing because you want it, with no case underneath it, is the wanting that got shut down early, and it's what to practise.

### How long before it comes back?

Longer than you would like and less time than you'd fear. It comes back when you start using it, and the first small want you say to another person does more than a month of thinking it over.

### What if what I want is unreasonable?

Then it's still information. Many of us have a stack of blockers sitting on top of wanting anything at all, and the reasonableness test is one of the heaviest. Want it on paper first. You can decide what to do about it afterwards, and you will be deciding with something in front of you rather than a blank.

## If this is you

If you're the one who can list what everybody around you needs, you already know how to pay close attention. What comes next is turning that attention onto yourself, and saying a want to somebody who could turn it down. That's where you find out whether it's yours or the one you inherited.

And that goes better with others around you, because a group does what a journal can't. There's a person there who might decline, and there's somebody watching who catches the shift in you when you ask, a few seconds before you notice it yourself.
