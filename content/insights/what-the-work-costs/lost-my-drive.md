A man in a group I run had thirteen years of winning in business.

Then it started going the other way, and after months of feeling it, he shared this with the group: "For the first time in my life, the drive's not there. Which is just a scary thing to say. And I hate it. I absolutely fucking hate it."

Two weeks before that, he'd been doing ten-hour days at a friend's company and barely felt them. So he had energy within him. It had stopped going to his own business, but he drew a conclusion about himself.

## I've lost my drive and I don't know why

Often the drive has gone for the work you have been pushing and became disconnected from everything else, so it feels like there is no drive for anything now. But it's still there, it's just become invisible, which is a different thing from the drive being depleted altogether. You find out which one you have by spending a day doing something completely different and seeing how you feel then at the end of it. Many of us never get that far, because a conclusion about ourselves is already locked before questioning another assumption.

That first conclusion is the easiest to jump to because of how long that drive had been the identity. He said it himself: "There's that lack of drive, which was, again, a bit of an identity for me, that's been taken away."

If being driven is who you've been for years, then when the drive stops, it feels like you've stopped too.

## What causes a person to have no ambition?

Usually the work stopped meaning anything to you a while before the energy disappeared, but often energy is produced by the meaning work provides. So when the work stops meaning anything, the ambition for it goes too.

For the guy I mentioned at the start, the fear came fast. "Am I going to become one of those, you know, lazy slobs on the sofa that's on benefits?" He knew it was a bit catastrophising, he said so himself, and the fear was still there.

He kept saying he needed to take action, even though for the first time in his life he felt like a zombie. Once we slowed it down, he could see that, and said he'd probably wanted to go back to exactly what got him to this position in the first place.

## What do you find out by spending a day doing something completely different?

Whether your drive is depleted, or just disconnected from the work you've been pushing. You don't have to think your way to the answer. Go and do something completely different, and see how you feel at the end of it.

A friend had asked him to help out at his company for a few weeks. His first instinct was no. He was barely getting anything done in his own business, and he didn't want to show up somewhere else and be bad at it compared to what he knew he could do. He went anyway.

He called it testing "this Daniel hypothesis of, right, is this a burnout problem, or is this an actual capacity problem?" Two weeks in, he reported back: "I've been doing ten-hour shifts and barely batting an eyelid."

He'd been pushing through his own work for eighteen months or more by then, out of fear, and getting less from it every month. So the capacity was fine. He was disengaged from his business.

| What you might call it | What it looks like | How you find out |
|---|---|---|
| Depleted | The energy is low wherever you put it, including things you used to enjoy | Spend a day doing something completely different, and see how you feel at the end of it |
| Disconnected from that work | The energy comes back as soon as it's pointed at something else | Ten-hour days elsewhere that you barely feel |
| Something medical | Flat for months, across everything, and nothing lifting it | See a doctor |

This page is about the first two rows. If it's everything, if it's been months, and nothing you try makes any difference, go and see a doctor.

## Where has my drive gone?

Often it's still there, just not in the hours you spend on that work. With many of the men I work with, it looks less dramatic. A decent job, decent money, and a day spent watching the clock.

One of them shared this: "I feel like I'm just wasting my life away at the moment. Where I'm, like, rushing the day, like, looking at the clock. 'I can't wait to get out of here, so I can go and do the stuff that I enjoy.' Because I don't enjoy those, you know, 9, 10 hours that I'm at work."

The drive hasn't gone. It's gone for the work you've been pushing. He's got energy for the stuff he enjoys, and he says so himself. Then he described a perfect day off, and in the next breath said his job doesn't allow it.

I've been here myself. I did everything productive I could think of, it wasn't helping, and I had no idea why. I'd also stopped wanting things for so long that when somebody asked me what I wanted, I didn't have an answer. That's where this goes if you leave it.

## What to do when you've lost your drive

Practise being unproductive, on purpose, without the usual distractions to get you through it. I gave the guy with the thirteen years a month of it. That was the number for him.

I said to him, "Lean into that feeling of being a lazy slob doing nothing, for a change. Because it's something that is so unfamiliar to you. You probably don't know how to do it. So practise it."

No PlayStation either, because that's how he'd usually escape the discomfort, and sitting in the discomfort is what he was practising. Longer walks instead, sit in the forest and listen to the birds, dream like a child again. He said it goes against everything he's done, and I asked him, why practise something you're already good at?

Two weeks later he told us how it went. Lying still, he went almost insane. Then he made a coffee without the audiobook on and quite enjoyed it, and went on a bike ride he'd never have gone on before. He said, "It's really weird, when I actually don't numb things out, when I focus on a task, you see more of things around you, things that you're doing, that you actually haven't seen before."

He also said, "From a financial point of view, I'm okay. It's more from a fear of 'I'm wasting precious time.' I think that's what's driving me to it."

So I asked him, the bike ride, noticing the little things, the appreciation, is that wasting time? He said, "No. That's not wasting time." So he had the answer.

Doing nothing is still doing something. It's the choice of not doing the other thing.

And notice when your best ideas come. Usually in the shower or driving, not in the middle of doing. He said it himself: "I've had a lot of realisations since I have stopped."

## Common questions

### Why do I have no ambition anymore?

Usually because the work stopped meaning something to you, and the ambition went with it. In my experience it does come back, pointed at something else. It just doesn't tell you when, so the time in between can feel like it's gone for good.

### Is having no drive a sign of depression?

It can be, and that's a conversation to have with a doctor. This page is about something more specific. The energy is still there for other things, it's just gone for the work you've been pushing. If it's flat across everything, for months, and nothing is lifting it, that's the other conversation, and it's worth having.

### Is doing nothing the same as giving up?

No. Doing nothing is a choice, you're choosing not to do the other thing. And if you've spent years producing, it might be one of the harder choices you've made in a while.

### Is this the same as needing a day off?

Not quite. A day off ends, and the same work is there on Monday. [Do I give myself permission to take a day off?](/insights/being-the-capable-one/do-i-give-myself-permission-to-take-a-day-off/) is about the day off itself. This page is about when the work has stopped meaning anything to you, and a day off won't change that.

## If this resonates

If you've spent years being the one who delivers, you already know how to show up whatever you feel like on the day. That's still within you, the same way it was on those ten-hour days at a friend's company. What comes next is being unproductive on purpose for long enough to figure out what you'd still want, even if no-one was counting the output.

And it goes better with a few others around you who'll ask what you did with your week, even when the answer is not much, and who are interested in what you're passionate about.
