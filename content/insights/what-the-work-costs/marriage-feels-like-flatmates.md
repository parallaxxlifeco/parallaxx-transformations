A man I was working with had been with his partner for decades. When I asked him what it was like at the start, he shared, "It was everything. Nothing else mattered." He could look at her and forget to breathe. "Take my breath away," he said. And he had no money, no prospects, no skills at the time, and still felt like "the world was my oyster. Our oyster."

"And then life happens." When I asked him about now, he said, "You just get into a routine of doing stuff, and it's almost kind of like being flatmates, or being really good friends. And it shouldn't be that way for either of us."

## Why does my marriage feel like we are roommates?

Often because for years the work got the focus, and the relationship got whatever was left over. The love is usually still there. You just stop having time together that isn't about running the house or the business, and at some point you're living alongside each other more than with each other.

He described his week like this: "I go to work, come home and do what I do. So there's no us time. She cooks dinner, I eat, I go to bed. Rinse and repeat the next day."

He didn't want to call the relationship stale. "Neglected would be a better word. It's almost like now it's just going through the motions. There's no excitement." And in the same conversation, "I still couldn't live without her."

He defended the relationship more than once while he was describing it. "I'm not saying it's bad, because it's not bad. Don't get the wrong idea. It's just neglected." I believe him.

You may have heard the quote, "Where your focus goes, the energy flows." For him the business took the focus for decades, and the relationship faded behind it.

## What were you providing for?

Usually for time together, and then you spent that time earning it. He said it himself: "I always thought that unless I could provide the means for us to do all that sort of shit, then none of that would happen anyway. So I leaned into the business to allow us to do that. But then we didn't do it because I was too much in the business."

He shared that one of his proudest things, over all those years, was that his partner hadn't needed to work for a very long time. "I consider it one of my biggest achievements." And in the same breath, "she doesn't see me."

He was proudest of the very thing that had turned them into flatmates. He earned the money so they could have time together, and earning it took the time.

Another man in one of my groups shared the same thinking about becoming a father, "I need to provide XYZ before I'm allowed to be a father," and called it bullshit once he could see it.

Many of the men I work with show love this way. Providing feels safer than asking for time with someone, and it looks like love from the outside, so it can go on for years before either of you mentions it.

## What are your conversations about?

Usually what happened today and what needs to happen tomorrow. A couple of weeks after he called it flatmates, I shared with him how I try to go out for dinner in the evenings and notice our daily achievements, what we're celebrating, what we're excited about. He went quiet for a moment, and then said,

"We always have conversations about what's happening at work, what needs to happen. We always have these conversations. But when I think about it, we only talk about what I'm doing, and not how she's doing or what she's doing."

And then, "It's all about me and what I'm doing. I just realised that now."

I see the same thing in the groups. Men come in and share their week like a to-do list, this happened, that happened, then this thing, an action list of how they lived their lives, with the feeling left out. At home it sounds the same, right? And the person across the table gets the report rather than you.

His first idea was that they needed to do more things together. I asked him, "What if you don't even need to do more stuff together right away? You just bring a curiosity to her. What she's been up to, how she's doing, what her thoughts are, what her fears are."

He thought about it and said, "We don't have to physically go anywhere or do anything. Just need to spend time together. Even if it's just going for a coffee."

Two weeks earlier he'd described the routine. Now he could hear what was missing from what he told her each evening, which was her. He still provides, and he's still proud of it. And now he wanted to ask about her day before telling her about his.

## How to get out of roommate phase with wife

Lead by being the exciting one. Create the feelings you loved at the start, show up as your best self, and invite your partner into that, rather than trying to sacrifice more and manufacture it. As I said to him, "That's probably what she was attracted to in the beginning."

And the good news is, you know how to do this relationship, because you've done it before. He could describe the start of his in detail, and he's still the man who did all of that.

I'll put my hand up, I want this in my own relationship too. "Four years in, I want to feel this excitement in the relationship continuing." That's mine to create. "The other person can't take care of that, but she does need to be able to lean into this experience to help me co-create it." So I go first, and she leans in.

| At the start | Years later | What he chose to bring back |
|---|---|---|
| "Nothing else mattered" | "Going through the motions" | A date night a week, his idea |
| "What made me happy was making her happy" | "We only talk about what I'm doing" | Curiosity about her day, her thoughts, her fears |
| Hours on the phone talking about nothing at all | "It might be a few days before I send her a message" | Two phone calls a week while they were apart |

He came up with those himself. "Something as simple as a, sounds weird, but a date night a week. That would be a start. No matter what happens, this is what we're doing. This is our night. Shut everything else off. That's us." And, "Have a cut-off point. End my day... at a certain time, so that we have even a couple of hours a night just being present with each other."

When they were going to be apart for a month, it would have been easy to let himself off the hook until they were back together. So I suggested two dedicated phone calls a week, "like how it was when you first started... when you could spend hours and hours together on the phone talking about nothing at all." His first answer was, "If I could pull off one phone call a week, that's progress for me." Then he committed to two. "That would be massive for me. That's just not who I am, you know."

And things go pretty fast with intention.

## Common questions

### What is roommate syndrome?

It's what people call a long relationship where you still get on, still run the house well together, and have stopped sharing much beyond the logistics. In my experience it comes on slowly, usually while one or both of you are very busy providing, so it can be years before anyone says the word flatmates.

### Does it mean we've fallen out of love?

Not necessarily. He said, "I still couldn't live without her," in the same conversation where he said they were like flatmates. The love was there. The time and the attention had gone into the business.

### Is a date night enough?

It's a good start, and he said so himself. It depends on what you bring to it. A date night spent reporting on your week is the same routine at a restaurant. Bring the curiosity, ask about her, and bring some of the excitement you had at the start.

### Is this the same as wanting more connection in general?

It's close. [How do I get more connection in my life?](/insights/getting-what-you-wanted/how-do-i-get-more-connection-in-my-life/) is about connection across your life, and that the connection you have is often the connection you asked for. This page is about the relationship you've been providing for, and the time together you gave up to provide it.

## If this resonates

If you've spent years making sure the people you love are taken care of, you already know how to show up for them day after day, whatever else is going on. What comes next is giving that same care to the person you started all of this for, and being the one who brings the excitement back, rather than waiting for the routine to change on its own.

The man I worked with also shared that apart from his partner, he had no-one he could talk to about the relationship. That makes it a lot harder than it needs to be. It goes better with a few others around you doing the same in their own relationships, who'll ask how the date night went, and who'll talk about what they're passionate about as well as what they're working on.
