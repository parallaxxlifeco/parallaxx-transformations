A group member said something in the first week of one of my groups that I still think about: "Healing comes with so many goodbyes."

They went on to say that it leads to some of the loneliest times in our lives, where it feels like it would be easier to just put up with all of it and go back to dealing with things the way you always had, instead of holding the new standard you've set for yourself. And that it's just deeply, deeply lonely.

No-one in the group had voiced the cost for them until then. I've heard it from a lot of people since, and I went through it myself.

## Why does it get lonelier after the breakthrough?

Because when you change, the old way of living goes, and some of the people who fit it go too, before anything new has come. And the high of finally having done the thing fades within a few weeks. What's left is a new standard that feels like you're carrying alone, without anyone around doing the same thing, and that stretch is the lonely one.

The loneliest stretch comes after the change works, which is backwards from how it gets sold. Personal development sells the breakthrough, and the breakthrough is real, it's just short.

One participant made the hardest call of their year and spent the first week afterwards, in their words, surfing a wave. Motivated, inspired, and naively thinking that was it. Grieved, moved on, time for the next chapter. The week after, they'd been sad, borderline depressed, and it came as a shock.

I see this with many of the guys I work with after a breakup. First it's yeah, I'm free. Then usually it takes two to three weeks for the reality to hit. Growth runs the same way. You get the insight, or the retreat, or you finally have the conversation you'd been putting off for a year, and there's a lift. And then you go home.

Another participant shared that anyone can be enlightened on retreat, with all the breath work and the meditation and the sunshine and the sea. But then you go back home and you get the reality check.

## Why do I want to go back to how it was?

Because going back is the option that ends the loneliness straight away. The old way had people in it and a role you knew how to play. So the pull gets strongest right when the old thing has gone and the new one hasn't shown up yet, and putting up with it starts to look like the reasonable choice.

And your head helps you get there. One participant said that when the grief came in, their mind was quite clever at it. Maybe it should have been. Maybe we should still be together. All of that. What got them the clarity back was talking to their mates.

This stretch is hard partly because it's so undramatic. A group member described it as what's left after you've cleared out all the really big stuff: the residue at the bottom of the pot, the sand that's really hard to get into the dustpan. The big moments had other people around them. The residue you tend to clear up by yourself.

## Is it normal to feel lonely with people all around you?

Yes, and it's very common. The lonely stretch is about whether anyone around you knows where you're at right now. You can be surrounded all day and still have the answer be no-one, mostly because you haven't told them.

A group member shared that they're never on their own. Ever. And in the last five years they'd been more lonely than at any point in their life. What they wanted was small. Someone to come home to and say, ah, this happened today. Someone to make dinner tonight so they didn't have to think about it.

When you get underneath it, the want is usually that small, and it needs another person to do something.

## Why is it so hard to tell anyone you're lonely?

Because saying it means admitting you can't handle this by yourself, and for someone capable that feels off limits. So you keep busy, you keep it together, and the loneliness grows underneath the busyness until it's too big to hide from.

That was me. I had just enough going on to keep myself distracted from feeling lonely, until the loneliness grew so much that I couldn't hide from it any longer. And when I did finally say it, saying it was the hardest thing.

> Because that was, I think, the hardest thing for me. To acknowledge it and admit it to myself, and then say it to someone else. I don't know whether it's different from a man or from a woman, just to say that, like, I'm lonely.
> — Daniel Lawson

The reflex in this stretch is familiar to anyone who's been the capable one for a long time. One participant described it when someone they'd been counting on pulled back a little. Straight to: fine then, I don't need anyone, I'm good on my own.

Being able to handle it yourself is real and it's gotten you a long way. It's also the same not-asking the growth was meant to change, and in this stretch it shows up looking like strength.

## What changes this when you're in it?

Telling one person where you're at, as you are in that moment, and letting them contribute. It's the small tweaks that can influence the biggest changes. Going out to meet a pile of new people tends to go badly for a while, and doing more work on yourself adds to what you're already dealing with alone.

A group member made the case against going out pretty convincingly. You step outside the door to try and meet people, somebody strange shows up, and you go straight back home. So then I'll just sit and be lonely. I get it. What I've seen change this, though, is having told someone else.

| What it looks like | What's running underneath | What changes this |
|---|---|---|
| Keeping busy, starting the next thing | Just enough distraction to not feel it | Admitting the word lonely to yourself first |
| Thinking about going back to how it was | The old way had people and a role in it | Looking for the evidence of the change |
| Fine then, I don't need anyone | The old not-asking | Telling one person, and letting them contribute |
| Deciding the odds are too bad to bother | Protecting yourself from another let-down | A small, specific ask of someone |

Looking for evidence matters more than it looks. When you're in the lonely stretch, it's very easy to decide nothing changed at all.

> It's easy to go and have the change, not look for evidence of it, and claim that nothing happened. And I think that's bullshit. So I'm looking for the evidence of the change.
> — Daniel Lawson

The catch is that change is easier to see from the outside. A participant said exactly that to me once. They couldn't see much change in themselves, and they could see it clearly in me.

And the group member who shared about the goodbyes had a question they used on themselves when it got bad: "Who did come before me?" They got curious about the people in their family who'd been through hard stretches. Their way of putting it was that you go anywhere as the ten thousand people who came before you, so you're never on your own. That's a different kind of company, and it counts.

## Common questions

### How long does the lonely stretch last?

Somebody asked me this in a group and I couldn't give a clean answer. I wanted to say it was ages. It felt like a lifetime. And looking back, it might have been a week that felt like months. What I do know is that the more we want it to pass, the less it passes, and the more we lean in and let ourselves feel it, the more it moves.

### Should I just sit with it?

A participant asked that too. What else is there to do other than embrace it and sit in the sadness? Feel it, yes, fully. And tell someone you're in it while you do. Sitting with it by yourself is what makes it drag.

### Is it normal to lose friends when you change?

Some people will go. Usually without a big row, they just stop fitting and you stop fitting them. That's real and it's worth grieving. The lonelier stretch, for many people, is afterwards, when you've changed and no-one near you has caught up yet.

## If this resonates

If you've made a change and kept your standard while it got lonely, you already know how to keep going when no-one's watching. What comes next is letting someone in while you're still in it, rather than a year later. Sometimes I notice myself keeping busy instead of saying it.

And it goes better with people who aren't counting on you to be fine. They can see the change before you can, and you can tell them where you're at.
